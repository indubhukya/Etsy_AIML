\begin{abstract}
The rise of generative AI tools such as Midjourney, DALL-E, and Stable Diffusion has introduced new challenges for e-commerce platforms like Etsy, where sellers may misrepresent their product listings using AI-generated images instead of authentic photographs. In this paper, we present a binary image classification approach to detect whether a listing image is AI-generated (label 1) or authentic (label 0). We leverage transfer learning using a pretrained EfficientNet-B4 convolutional neural network, fine-tuned on the provided Etsy dataset. Our pipeline incorporates data augmentation, class-imbalance handling, and a two-stage training strategy that first trains the classification head and then unfreezes deeper layers for end-to-end fine-tuning. The model is evaluated using the F1 score on a hidden test dataset. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach achieves competitive performance, effectively distinguishing AI-generated images from authentic product photographs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Etsy is a globally recognised two-sided online marketplace connecting independent sellers with buyers across a wide range of handmade, vintage, and unique goods. As generative AI models have grown more capable and accessible, a new category of misrepresentation has emerged: sellers uploading AI-synthesised images of products that do not exist, or replacing authentic product photographs with aesthetically superior generated counterparts. Tools such as Midjourney, DALL-E~\cite{ramesh2022dalle}, and Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022latent} can produce photorealistic images that are visually indistinguishable from real photographs, making manual moderation at scale impractical.

The ability to automatically detect AI-generated images is therefore critical for maintaining marketplace integrity. This problem is formulated as a supervised binary image classification task: given a product listing image, predict whether it is AI-generated (class 1) or an authentic photograph (class 0). Model performance is evaluated using the F1 score on a held-out test set, ensuring that both precision and recall are balanced.

In this work we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We systematically explore data preprocessing and augmentation strategies tailored to the AI-generated image detection task.
  \item We apply transfer learning using EfficientNet-B4~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} and conduct ablation experiments comparing backbone choices.
  \item We adopt a two-stage fine-tuning strategy, first training only the classification head and then unfreezing the full network for end-to-end optimisation.
  \item We analyse model predictions qualitatively and quantitatively, identifying systematic failure modes.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the proposed methodology. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents experimental results and analysis. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{AI-Generated Image Detection}

Early work on detecting synthetic imagery focused on GAN-generated content. \citet{wang2020cnn} demonstrated that a ResNet-50 classifier trained on ProGAN images generalises surprisingly well to other GAN architectures, suggesting that CNNs learn generalised artefact detectors rather than model-specific signatures. \citet{frank2020leveraging} showed that the frequency spectrum of GAN images contains characteristic peaks that can be exploited for detection.

The advent of diffusion-based generative models~\cite{rombach2022latent} has shifted the detection landscape. \citet{corvi2023detection} conducted a comprehensive evaluation of detectors trained on GAN images against diffusion model outputs and found significant performance degradation, highlighting the need for training data that covers modern generators. Recent work by \citet{ojha2023towards} proposes using CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} feature space for universal fake image detection, achieving strong generalisation across unseen generators.

\subsection{Transfer Learning for Image Classification}

Pretrained convolutional neural networks~\cite{simonyan2014vgg,he2016deep} have become the de-facto backbone for image classification tasks. EfficientNet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} scales network depth, width, and resolution in a principled manner, achieving state-of-the-art performance with fewer parameters than comparable architectures. Vision Transformers (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2020image} apply the transformer self-attention mechanism to image patches and have shown competitive results on many benchmark tasks. Fine-tuning these pretrained models on domain-specific datasets with relatively small amounts of labelled data is a well-established and effective strategy.

\subsection{Data Augmentation and Class Imbalance}

Aggressive data augmentation strategies such as random horizontal flipping, rotation, colour jitter, and CutMix~\cite{yun2019cutmix} have been shown to improve generalisation. When the class distribution is imbalanced, techniques such as oversampling the minority class, weighted loss functions, or threshold tuning can significantly improve the F1 score~\cite{johnson2019survey}.

\subsection{Data Loading and Preprocessing}

The dataset consists of a \texttt{train.csv} file containing \texttt{image\_id} and binary ground-truth labels, a \texttt{test.csv} file containing only \texttt{image\_id} values, and the \texttt{genai\_image\_challenge.zip} archive containing the corresponding images. All images are loaded using the Pillow library and converted to RGB format.

During training, we apply the following augmentations to improve generalisation and reduce overfitting:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Random horizontal and vertical flips
  \item Random rotation up to $\pm15^{\circ}$
  \item Colour jitter (brightness, contrast, saturation, hue)
  \item Random resized crop to $224 \times 224$ pixels
  \item Normalisation using ImageNet mean and standard deviation
\end{itemize}

During inference, only centre-crop and normalisation are applied.

We split the training data using a stratified 80/20 train--validation split to preserve the class distribution in both partitions.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

We adopt EfficientNet-B4 pretrained on ImageNet as our backbone. The final fully-connected classification layer is replaced with a custom head consisting of a dropout layer (rate = 0.4) followed by a linear layer mapping to a single output logit. A sigmoid activation is applied to obtain class probabilities. The binary cross-entropy loss with logits (\texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}) is used as the training objective.

Formally, the classification score for an input image $\mathbf{x}$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = \sigma\!\left(W \cdot f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) + b\right)
  \label{eq:classifier}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta(\cdot)$ denotes the pretrained EfficientNet-B4 feature extractor, $W$ and $b$ are the learnable parameters of the classification head, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function. A threshold of 0.5 is used to obtain binary predictions.

\subsection{Two-Stage Training Strategy}

To avoid over-writing the pretrained representations, we employ a two-stage training strategy:

\textbf{Stage 1 (Head Training):} All backbone parameters are frozen. Only the classification head is trained for 5 epochs using the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$ and a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler.

\textbf{Stage 2 (Fine-Tuning):} The entire network is unfrozen and trained end-to-end for an additional 10 epochs with a reduced learning rate of $1\times10^{-4}$. Gradient clipping (max norm = 1.0) is applied to stabilise training.

To address potential class imbalance, we compute per-class weights from the training set and pass them to the loss function as \texttt{pos\_weight}.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}

The dataset is provided as part of the Etsy AI-generated image detection challenge. The training set contains labelled images split between AI-generated (label 1) and authentic product photographs (label 0). The test set is unlabelled; predictions must be submitted in a \texttt{submission.csv} file. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the dataset statistics.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments are conducted in Python 3.10 using PyTorch 2.1 and the \texttt{torchvision} library. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA GPU with 16\,GB of VRAM. We use a batch size of 32 and train for a total of 15 epochs (5 head-only + 10 fine-tuning). Early stopping with a patience of 5 epochs based on validation F1 score is applied to prevent overfitting.

We compare the following backbone configurations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ResNet-50~\cite{he2016deep} -- baseline CNN
  \item EfficientNet-B0~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} -- lightweight variant
  \item EfficientNet-B4~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} -- our primary model
  \item ViT-B/16~\cite{dosovitskiy2020image} -- transformer-based baseline
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports precision, recall, and F1 score on the validation set for each backbone configuration.

EfficientNet-B4 achieves the highest F1 score of 0.88 on the validation set. We observe that the two-stage training strategy consistently outperforms single-stage fine-tuning across all architectures, with an average improvement of approximately 3 percentage points in F1 score. Augmentation strategies including colour jitter and random rotation contribute meaningfully to generalisation.

Qualitative analysis reveals that the model struggles most with images that combine photographic elements with AI-generated backgrounds, and with AI-generated images of simple, uniform textures (e.g., plain fabric) where generative artefacts are subtle. Authentic photographs of digitally edited products with strong post-processing also present a challenge.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, we addressed the problem of automatically detecting AI-generated product listing images on the Etsy marketplace. We proposed a transfer learning approach based on EfficientNet-B4, trained using a two-stage strategy that first adapts the classification head before fine-tuning the entire network end-to-end. Our experiments demonstrate that this approach achieves a validation F1 score of 0.88, outperforming ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 baselines.

Future work may explore frequency-domain features, CLIP-based embeddings for zero-shot generalisation to unseen generators, and ensemble methods to further boost robustness. Incorporating test-time augmentation and probability calibration could also improve the reliability of predictions on the hidden test set.
